# Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

## Definition
GDP is the total monetary value of all finished goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific time period, typically one year or one quarter.

## Nominal vs. Real GDP
- **Nominal GDP** is measured in current prices and does not account for inflation.
- **Real GDP** adjusts for inflation using a base year, making comparisons across time meaningful.
- When comparing GDP across years, always prefer real GDP to avoid confusing price growth with output growth.

## GDP per Capita
GDP per capita divides total GDP by population. It is the most common proxy for average living standards. A country can have high total GDP but low per capita GDP if population is large (e.g., India vs. Norway).

## Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)
PPP-adjusted GDP accounts for price-level differences between countries. A dollar buys more in Kazakhstan than in Germany. PPP GDP is better for comparing welfare; nominal GDP is better for comparing market size.

## GDP Growth Rate
The annual % change in real GDP. Rates above 2-3% are generally considered healthy for developed economies. Emerging markets typically grow faster (4-7%). Sustained negative growth (two consecutive quarters) is called a recession.

## Limitations of GDP
- Does not capture income distribution (two countries can have the same GDP but very different inequality).
- Ignores informal economy, unpaid work, environmental degradation.
- High GDP growth can coexist with rising poverty if gains are concentrated.

## Reading GDP Charts
- Sudden drops often correspond to financial crises (2008-09) or pandemics (2020).
- V-shaped recoveries are common after short shocks; L-shaped recoveries indicate structural damage.
- Compare GDP growth rate, not absolute levels, when comparing countries of different sizes.
```

# Inflation

## Definition
Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises over time, reducing purchasing power. It is most commonly measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI).

## CPI vs. PPI
- **CPI** (Consumer Price Index): measures prices of a basket of consumer goods. Most relevant to households.
- **PPI** (Producer Price Index): measures prices at the wholesale/producer level. PPI changes often lead CPI changes by 1-3 months.

## Types of Inflation
- **Demand-pull**: too much money chasing too few goods. Common during economic booms.
- **Cost-push**: input costs (energy, labor, materials) rise, pushing prices up. Common during supply shocks.
- **Built-in**: wage-price spiral where workers demand higher wages to cover rising costs, which then raises costs further.

## Hyperinflation
Hyperinflation occurs when monthly inflation exceeds 50%. It destroys savings and destabilizes economies. Historical examples: Weimar Germany (1923), Zimbabwe (2007-08).

## Disinflation vs. Deflation
- **Disinflation**: inflation rate is falling but still positive (e.g., from 8% to 4%).
- **Deflation**: prices are actually falling (negative inflation). Deflation can cause consumers to delay purchases, slowing the economy.

## Relationship to Interest Rates
Central banks raise interest rates to fight inflation (makes borrowing expensive, reduces demand). This is the primary tool of monetary policy. There is typically a 12-18 month lag between rate changes and inflation effects.

## Interpreting Inflation Charts
- Spikes above 5-6% in developed economies signal policy response risk (rate hikes).
- Persistent inflation above target (usually 2%) suggests structural imbalances.
- Compare headline vs. core inflation (which excludes food and energy) for a cleaner signal.
```

# Unemployment

## Definition
The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is jobless, actively seeking work, and available to work. It does not count people who have stopped looking for work (discouraged workers).

## Types of Unemployment
- **Frictional**: temporary unemployment during job transitions. Normal and healthy.
- **Structural**: mismatch between worker skills and available jobs (e.g., automation displacing factory workers).
- **Cyclical**: caused by economic downturns — demand for labor falls when GDP contracts.
- **Seasonal**: predictable fluctuations (e.g., tourism, agriculture).

## Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)
The rate at which inflation is stable — neither accelerating nor decelerating. Typically 4-5% in developed economies. Unemployment below NAIRU can trigger wage inflation.

## Okun's Law
An empirical relationship: for every 1% GDP falls below potential, unemployment rises by about 0.5%. Used to estimate the economic cost of recessions.

## Youth Unemployment
Youth unemployment (ages 15-24) is typically 2-3x the overall rate. High youth unemployment signals structural barriers to labor market entry and has long-term scarring effects on lifetime earnings.

## Limitations
- Underemployment (part-time workers who want full-time work) is not captured.
- Labor force participation rate matters: a falling unemployment rate due to people leaving the workforce is not genuine improvement.

## Reading Unemployment Charts
- Sharp spikes followed by slow recovery are common in recessions (2009, 2020).
- Persistently high unemployment (above 10%) in developed economies suggests structural problems.
- Compare unemployment rate with labor force participation rate together for full picture.
```

# Gini Coefficient and Lorenz Curve

## Lorenz Curve
The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income (or wealth) received by the bottom x% of the population. If income were perfectly equally distributed, the curve would be a 45-degree diagonal line (line of perfect equality). Real Lorenz curves bow below this diagonal.

## Gini Coefficient
The Gini coefficient measures the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality, expressed as a ratio between 0 and 1 (or 0-100%). 
- **0** = perfect equality (everyone has the same income)
- **1** = perfect inequality (one person has all income)

## Interpretation Ranges
- **< 0.25**: Very equal (Scandinavian countries)
- **0.25 - 0.35**: Moderately equal (most of Western Europe)
- **0.35 - 0.45**: Moderate inequality (USA ≈ 0.39, Russia ≈ 0.37)
- **0.45 - 0.55**: High inequality (many Latin American and African countries)
- **> 0.55**: Very high inequality (South Africa ≈ 0.63)

## Limitations
- Two countries can have the same Gini but very different poverty rates.
- Gini is insensitive to where in the distribution inequality occurs.
- Data quality varies significantly — informal economies are often underrepresented.
- Pre-tax vs. post-tax Gini differs substantially; market Gini vs. disposable income Gini can differ by 0.1+ points.

## GDP and Gini Paradox
Economic growth (rising GDP) does not automatically reduce inequality. Many fast-growing economies have seen rising Gini coefficients simultaneously, especially in early industrialization phases (Kuznets curve hypothesis).

## Reading Lorenz Curve Charts
- The further the curve bows from the diagonal, the more unequal the distribution.
- Crossing Lorenz curves mean one distribution is not unambiguously more equal than another — Gini alone cannot rank them.
```

# Trade Balance and Current Account

## Trade Balance
The trade balance is the difference between a country's exports and imports of goods and services. A **trade surplus** means exports > imports; a **trade deficit** means imports > exports.

## Current Account
The current account is a broader measure that includes:
- Trade in goods (merchandise trade)
- Trade in services
- Net income from abroad (dividends, wages)
- Current transfers (remittances, foreign aid)

## Interpreting Trade Data
- A persistent trade deficit is not always negative — it may reflect strong domestic demand or an attractive investment environment.
- Small open economies often run deficits and finance them with capital inflows.
- Resource-rich countries (oil exporters) typically run surpluses.

## Relationship to Exchange Rates
- A weaker currency makes exports cheaper and imports more expensive, tending to reduce deficits over time (J-curve effect: short-term worsening before improvement, typically 6-18 months).
- Currency appreciation has the opposite effect.

## Terms of Trade
The ratio of export prices to import prices. Improvement in terms of trade (export prices rise relative to import prices) increases national income. Commodity-dependent economies are vulnerable to terms of trade shocks.

## Reading Trade Charts
- Sudden deficit widening often follows currency appreciation or an economic boom (rising imports).
- Surplus countries (Germany, China) often face political pressure from trading partners.
- Compare trade balance as % of GDP rather than absolute values for cross-country comparisons.
```

# Demographics

## Population Growth Rate
The annual % change in population, determined by births, deaths, and net migration. Positive growth increases the labor supply and can boost GDP growth. Negative growth (declining population) creates fiscal pressure through pension and healthcare systems.

## Dependency Ratio
The ratio of non-working age population (children + elderly) to working-age population.
- **Youth dependency ratio**: population under 15 / working-age population.
- **Old-age dependency ratio**: population over 65 / working-age population.
- High and rising old-age dependency ratios are a fiscal challenge for developed economies (Japan, Germany, Kazakhstan by 2040s).

## Demographic Dividend
When a country transitions from high to low birth rates, there is a period where the working-age share of the population peaks. This "demographic dividend" can fuel GDP growth if institutions are strong enough to absorb the workforce.

## Aging Economies
Economies with median age above 40 (Japan, Italy, Germany) face: slower labor force growth, higher pension spending, potential deflation from lower consumption. Immigration can offset aging but creates political challenges.

## Migration Effects
Immigration increases labor supply, can fill skill gaps, and tends to increase GDP. The fiscal impact depends heavily on immigrant skill levels and age distribution.

## Reading Demographic Charts
- Population pyramids that are wide at the top (many elderly, few young) signal fiscal pressure.
- Countries with high population growth but low GDP per capita face a "youth bulge" — large youth cohorts that require investment in education and job creation.
```

# Reading Economic Charts

## Time Series Charts
Time series charts show a variable over time. Key things to check:
- **Trend**: is the overall direction up, down, or flat?
- **Volatility**: are fluctuations large or small relative to the trend?
- **Breakpoints**: are there sudden level shifts? These often correspond to crises, policy changes, or data revisions.
- **Seasonality**: quarterly data often shows seasonal patterns (retail sales spike in Q4).

## Identifying Anomalies
- Values that deviate more than 2 standard deviations from the trend deserve investigation.
- Flat periods followed by sudden jumps can indicate data collection problems or methodological changes.
- Missing values (gaps) may reflect political instability, data suppression, or genuine absence.

## Correlation vs. Causation
Two indicators moving together does not mean one causes the other. For example, ice cream sales and drowning deaths are correlated (both peak in summer) but causally unrelated. Always ask: is there a plausible causal mechanism?

## Cross-Country Comparisons
- Always check whether data is comparable (same definition, same source, same year).
- GDP comparisons should use PPP-adjusted figures for welfare comparisons, nominal for market size.
- Some indicators (e.g., unemployment) are measured differently across countries.

## Data Gaps and Revisions
- Economic data is frequently revised. Preliminary figures can change substantially.
- GDP data has three rounds: advance, second, and final estimate.
- World Bank data often has a 1-2 year lag for developing economies.

## Confidence in Trends
- Short time series (< 10 years) are unreliable for identifying trends.
- A trend visible in 5+ years of data is more meaningful than a 2-year movement.
- Always consider the confidence interval around any statistical estimate.
```

# Forecasting Limitations

## Why Forecasts Fail
Economic forecasts are inherently uncertain because:
- Economies are complex adaptive systems with feedback loops.
- Human behavior changes in response to forecasts (self-fulfilling/defeating prophecies).
- Structural breaks (crises, wars, pandemics) are impossible to predict from historical data.
- Data used to build models is revised after the fact.

## Types of Uncertainty
- **Parameter uncertainty**: model coefficients are estimated from limited data.
- **Model uncertainty**: the true model of the economy is unknown.
- **Scenario uncertainty**: future policy decisions and exogenous shocks are unknown.

## Confidence Intervals
Forecasts should always be presented with confidence intervals. A 95% confidence interval means: if the model is correct and we repeated the forecast many times, 95% of intervals would contain the true value. Intervals widen rapidly over the forecast horizon.

## Black Swans
Rare, high-impact events (financial crises, pandemics, wars) that lie outside normal model assumptions. No statistical model trained on past data can reliably predict them. This is why point forecasts 5+ years out are almost always wrong in magnitude.

## Model Assumptions
Time-series models (ARIMA, exponential smoothing) assume:
- The future will resemble the past (stationarity).
- Relationships between variables are stable.
- No structural breaks in the data.

These assumptions frequently fail in practice.

## How to Use Forecasts
- Use forecasts to understand likely direction, not precise magnitude.
- Use multiple models and scenarios rather than a single point forecast.
- Assign higher credibility to near-term forecasts (1-2 years) than long-term ones (5+ years).
- Always check forecast track record for the specific model and data source.
```

# Relationships Between Economic Indicators

## Phillips Curve
The Phillips curve describes a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment: lower unemployment tends to come with higher inflation (tight labor markets push up wages). In the 1970s stagflation (high inflation + high unemployment), the original Phillips curve broke down. The modern New Keynesian Phillips curve uses "expected" inflation and the "output gap."

## Kuznets Curve
Simon Kuznets hypothesized that inequality (Gini) first rises as countries industrialize, then falls as they become richer — forming an inverted-U shape. Empirical support is mixed; many fast-growing economies see continuously rising inequality.

## GDP and Gini (Growth-Inequality Paradox)
High GDP growth does not guarantee falling inequality. Technology-driven growth often benefits skilled workers disproportionately. Redistribution through fiscal policy (taxes, transfers) can offset market-driven inequality increases.

## Inflation and Interest Rates (Fisher Effect)
Nominal interest rate ≈ Real interest rate + Expected inflation. Central banks set nominal rates; when inflation rises above target, they raise rates to cool the economy. Higher rates reduce investment and consumption, slowing growth.

## Unemployment and GDP (Okun's Law)
A 1% increase in cyclical unemployment corresponds to roughly 2% of lost GDP relative to potential. Recessions (negative GDP growth) consistently cause unemployment spikes with a 1-2 quarter lag.

## Trade Balance and GDP
Domestic economic booms increase imports (higher consumer spending), worsening the trade balance. Recessions reduce imports, improving the balance. The exchange rate mediates this relationship.

## Population Growth and GDP per Capita
Fast population growth can dilute per capita GDP even if total GDP rises. Countries with rapid demographic growth need even faster economic growth to improve living standards. The demographic dividend (see demographics.md) can provide a temporary boost.

## Key Analytical Combinations
When analyzing a country, these indicator pairs reveal the most:
- **GDP growth + Gini**: are gains being shared?
- **Inflation + Unemployment**: where is the economy in the cycle?
- **Trade balance + Exchange rate**: external competitiveness.
- **Population growth + GDP per capita**: long-term welfare trajectory.
```

from __future__ import annotations

import logging
import re
from pathlib import Path
from typing import Optional

import numpy as np

logger = logging.getLogger(__name__)

_model = None
_chunks: list[str] = []
_index: Optional[np.ndarray] = None  # shape (N, D)

_CHUNK_SIZE = 300   # tokens (approx chars / 4)
_CHUNK_OVERLAP = 50
_MODEL_NAME = "all-MiniLM-L6-v2"


def _load_model():
    global _model
    if _model is not None:
        return _model
    from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
    logger.info("rag: loading embedding model %s", _MODEL_NAME)
    _model = SentenceTransformer(_MODEL_NAME)
    logger.info("rag: model loaded")
    return _model


def _split_chunks(text: str, size: int = _CHUNK_SIZE, overlap: int = _CHUNK_OVERLAP) -> list[str]:
    """Split text into overlapping word-based chunks."""
    words = text.split()
    if not words:
        return []
    chunks = []
    step = size - overlap
    for i in range(0, len(words), step):
        chunk = " ".join(words[i : i + size])
        if chunk:
            chunks.append(chunk)
    return chunks


def build_index(kb_dir: str | Path) -> None:
    """Read all .md files in kb_dir, split into chunks, embed, store index."""
    global _chunks, _index

    kb_path = Path(kb_dir)
    if not kb_path.exists():
        logger.warning("rag: knowledge_base dir %s does not exist — RAG disabled", kb_dir)
        _chunks = []
        _index = None
        return

    raw_chunks: list[str] = []
    for md_file in sorted(kb_path.glob("*.md")):
        try:
            text = md_file.read_text(encoding="utf-8")
            # Strip markdown headers for embedding, keep content
            text = re.sub(r"^#+\s+", "", text, flags=re.MULTILINE)
            file_chunks = _split_chunks(text)
            raw_chunks.extend(file_chunks)
            logger.debug("rag: indexed %d chunks from %s", len(file_chunks), md_file.name)
        except Exception as exc:
            logger.warning("rag: failed to read %s: %s", md_file, exc)

    if not raw_chunks:
        logger.warning("rag: no chunks found in %s", kb_dir)
        _chunks = []
        _index = None
        return

    model = _load_model()
    embeddings = model.encode(raw_chunks, show_progress_bar=False)
    # Normalize for cosine similarity via dot product
    norms = np.linalg.norm(embeddings, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    norms = np.where(norms == 0, 1.0, norms)
    _index = (embeddings / norms).astype(np.float32)
    _chunks = raw_chunks
    logger.info("rag: index built — %d chunks from %s", len(_chunks), kb_dir)


def retrieve(query: str, top_k: int = 4) -> list[str]:
    """Return top_k most relevant chunks for the query."""
    if _index is None or not _chunks:
        return []
    model = _load_model()
    q_emb = model.encode([query], show_progress_bar=False)[0]
    norm = np.linalg.norm(q_emb)
    if norm == 0:
        return []
    q_emb = (q_emb / norm).astype(np.float32)
    scores = _index @ q_emb
    top_indices = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:top_k]
    return [_chunks[i] for i in top_indices]
```

def _call_openrouter(messages: list[dict], system_prompt: str) -> str:
    if not OPENROUTER_API_KEY:
        raise RuntimeError("OPENROUTER_API_KEY is not configured")
    with httpx.Client(timeout=OPENROUTER_TIMEOUT_SECONDS) as client:
        response = client.post(

                "temperature": 0.3,

                "messages": [{"role": "system", "content": system_prompt}, *messages],
            },
        )
        response.raise_for_status()
        body = response.json()
    choices = body.get("choices") or []
    if not choices:
        raise RuntimeError("OpenRouter returned no choices")
    content = (choices[0].get("message") or {}).get("content", "").strip()
    if not content:
        raise RuntimeError("OpenRouter returned empty content")
    return content

def _parse_structured(text: str) -> tuple[str, list[str], str, list[str]]:
    try:
        clean = re.sub(r"```(?:json)?\s*|\s*```", "", text.strip())
        data = json.loads(clean)
        return (
            str(data.get("summary", text[:300])),
            [str(x) for x in data.get("insights", [])],
            str(data.get("limitations", "")),
            [str(x) for x in data.get("suggested_next_steps", [])],
        )
    except (json.JSONDecodeError, AttributeError, ValueError):
        return text, [], "", []


def _parse_suggestions(text: str) -> list[str]:
    try:
        clean = re.sub(r"```(?:json)?\s*|\s*```", "", text.strip())
        result = json.loads(clean)
        if isinstance(result, list):
            return [str(s) for s in result[:5]]
    except (json.JSONDecodeError, ValueError):
        pass
    lines = [ln.strip().lstrip("•-0123456789. ") for ln in text.split("\n") if ln.strip()]
    return [ln for ln in lines if ln][:5]

def _format_context(context_snapshot: Optional[dict]) -> str:
    if not context_snapshot:
        return ""
    parts = []

    indicators = context_snapshot.get("indicators") or []
    start = context_snapshot.get("start_year")
    end = context_snapshot.get("end_year")
    if countries:
        parts.append(f"Selected countries: {', '.join(countries)}")
    if indicators:
        parts.append(f"Selected indicators: {', '.join(indicators)}")
    if start or end:
        parts.append(f"Year range: {start or '?'} - {end or '?'}")
    if not parts:
        return ""
    return "\n\n[Chart Context]\n" + "\n".join(parts)

def chat(
    user_id: int,
    conversation_id: Optional[int],
    message: str,
    context_snapshot: Optional[dict],
    language: str,
    db: Session,
) -> StructuredAIResponse:

    conv = _get_or_create_conversation(user_id, conversation_id, message, db)
    history = _load_history(conv.id, db)
    rag_text = _format_rag(chunks)
    ctx_text = _format_context(context_snapshot)

import React from "react";

function formatRelative(isoString) {
  if (!isoString) return "";
  const date = new Date(isoString);
  const now = new Date();
  const diffMs = now - date;
  const diffMin = Math.floor(diffMs / 60000);
  if (diffMin < 1) return "just now";
  if (diffMin < 60) return `${diffMin}m ago`;
  const diffHr = Math.floor(diffMin / 60);
  if (diffHr < 24) return `${diffHr}h ago`;
  const diffDays = Math.floor(diffHr / 24);
  return `${diffDays}d ago`;
}

# AI Economic Mentor — Architecture

## Why RAG Instead of Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning requires a large, curated training dataset of (question, answer) pairs about economic indicators — which we do not have. It is expensive (GPU hours), brittle (the model must be retrained whenever knowledge changes), and opaque (it is hard to audit what the model "learned").

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is preferable because:
1. **No training data required** — knowledge lives in editable Markdown files.
2. **Updatable without retraining** — add a new `.md` file and call `/api/v1/ai/reindex`.
3. **Transparent** — every response includes `rag_chunks_used`, showing exactly what context was retrieved.
4. **Accurate grounding** — the LLM is given explicit context, reducing hallucination on domain-specific facts.

## Embedding Index

At FastAPI startup, `app/services/rag.py` reads all `knowledge_base/*.md` files, splits them into ~300-word overlapping chunks (50-word overlap), and embeds each chunk using `sentence-transformers` `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`. The resulting embeddings are normalized and stored as a numpy matrix in memory.

At query time, the user's question is embedded with the same model. Cosine similarity is computed via a single matrix–vector dot product (`index @ q_embedding`). The top-4 chunks are injected into the LLM prompt as `[Knowledge Base]` context.

No vector database is needed for a knowledge base of ~9 files (~100 chunks). Total index size is under 1 MB.

## Session Memory

`AIConversation` and `AIMessage` (SQLAlchemy models in `models.py`) store per-user conversation history in the existing FastAPI database (SQLite in dev, PostgreSQL in Docker).

On every LLM call, the last 6 messages from the active conversation are loaded and included in the prompt as conversation history. This gives the LLM short-term context without overflowing the context window.

The `context_snapshot` field on `AIConversation` stores the most recent chart state (countries, indicators, year range) passed by the frontend.

## Data Flow

```
User → POST /api/v1/ai/chat
         │
         ├─ Retrieve top-4 RAG chunks (rag.retrieve)
         ├─ Load last 6 messages from DB (AIMessage)
         ├─ Build LLM prompt:
         │    system: "You are an AI Economic Mentor..."
         │    [Knowledge Base] chunks
         │    [Chart Context] (countries, indicators, years)
         │    conversation history (last 6 messages)
         │    user message
         │
         ├─ POST https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/chat/completions
         │    model: meta-llama/llama-3.3-70b-instruct:free
         │
         ├─ Parse structured JSON response
         │    { summary, insights[], limitations, suggested_next_steps[] }
         │
         ├─ Save AIMessage (user + assistant) to DB
         ├─ Update AIConversation.updated_at
         │
         └─ Return StructuredAIResponse to frontend
```

## Why OpenRouter

OpenRouter provides an OpenAI-compatible `/chat/completions` API that routes requests to dozens of models, including free tiers (Llama 3.3 70B, Gemma, Mistral). This means:
- One integration covers many models — switch by changing `OPENROUTER_MODEL` in `.env`.
- Free tier available for development and demonstration.
- No vendor lock-in — the integration code is identical to OpenAI's API.

## Provider Fallback

If `OPENROUTER_API_KEY` is not set, the AI mentor endpoints return HTTP 500 with a clear error message. The existing `POST /api/v1/analytics/chart/explain` endpoint continues to work independently using its own provider configuration (`CHART_EXPLAIN_PROVIDER`).

## Auth Enforcement

All `/api/v1/ai/*` endpoints (except `/reindex`) use the `require_agreement` FastAPI dependency, which:
1. Validates the JWT access token (local signature check).
2. Introspects the live `agreement_accepted` status from Django via `POST /api/auth/introspect`.
3. Rejects with HTTP 403 if the user has not accepted the agreement.

The `/reindex` endpoint requires `require_staff` (role must be `staff` or `admin`).

## Future Extensions

The architecture is designed for incremental upgrades:
- **Streaming**: replace `httpx.Client` with `httpx.stream()` and add an SSE endpoint.
- **Multi-agent**: add a `tool_calls` loop around `_call_openrouter` to invoke `rag.retrieve` or `_build_data_summary` as tools.
- **Better RAG**: swap the numpy index for a vector database (Chroma, pgvector) when the knowledge base grows beyond ~1000 chunks.
- **Personalization**: add user preferences and history summaries to the system prompt.
