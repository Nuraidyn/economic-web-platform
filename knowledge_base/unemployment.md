# Unemployment

## Definition
The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is jobless, actively seeking work, and available to work. It does not count people who have stopped looking for work (discouraged workers).

## Types of Unemployment
- **Frictional**: temporary unemployment during job transitions. Normal and healthy.
- **Structural**: mismatch between worker skills and available jobs (e.g., automation displacing factory workers).
- **Cyclical**: caused by economic downturns — demand for labor falls when GDP contracts.
- **Seasonal**: predictable fluctuations (e.g., tourism, agriculture).

## Natural Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)
The rate at which inflation is stable — neither accelerating nor decelerating. Typically 4-5% in developed economies. Unemployment below NAIRU can trigger wage inflation.

## Okun's Law
An empirical relationship: for every 1% GDP falls below potential, unemployment rises by about 0.5%. Used to estimate the economic cost of recessions.

## Youth Unemployment
Youth unemployment (ages 15-24) is typically 2-3x the overall rate. High youth unemployment signals structural barriers to labor market entry and has long-term scarring effects on lifetime earnings.

## Limitations
- Underemployment (part-time workers who want full-time work) is not captured.
- Labor force participation rate matters: a falling unemployment rate due to people leaving the workforce is not genuine improvement.

## Reading Unemployment Charts
- Sharp spikes followed by slow recovery are common in recessions (2009, 2020).
- Persistently high unemployment (above 10%) in developed economies suggests structural problems.
- Compare unemployment rate with labor force participation rate together for full picture.
